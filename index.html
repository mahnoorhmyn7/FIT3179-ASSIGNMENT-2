01 / THE CONSERVATION CHALLENGE

Australia's wildlife is under pressure

Australia's threatened-species records identify animals facing different levels of extinction risk. Understanding their distribution helps explain why conservation programs matter.

FIGURE 01 • CHOROPLETH MAP

Where are threatened animals listed?

Explore the number of listed taxa across Australian states and territories. A taxon can appear in multiple states.

KEY FINDING
State differences are substantial

NSW has 287 threatened-taxa occurrence records, compared with 52 in the ACT. These totals describe recorded occurrences, not population size.

These are state occurrence flags among 700 nationally listed taxa, not wild animal population counts or independently verified state threat lists.

FIGURE 02 • HEATMAP

Which animal groups face the greatest threats?

Compare the distribution of national conservation categories across animal groups.

KEY FINDING
Extinction risk spans many animal groups

Of the 700 listed taxa, 255 are Endangered, 221 Vulnerable and 149 Critically Endangered. Look across the heatmap to see how these categories vary by class.

Source: Australian Government threatened-species data, extracted 28 August 2026.

FIGURE 03 • TREEMAP

Which animal groups make up Australia's threatened wildlife?

Each rectangle represents an animal class. Its area shows the number of nationally listed threatened taxa belonging to that class. Larger rectangles represent more listed taxa.

KEY FINDING
Birds and mammals dominate the list

Birds (Aves) account for 186 listed taxa and mammals (Mammalia) for 152. Treemap area represents listed taxa, not abundance in the wild.

Source: Australian Government threatened-species records. Animal classes are scientific taxonomic categories. Small groups may be visible only through tooltips.

02 / BEYOND THE ENCLOSURE

From zoo care to conservation action

Zoos can contribute to conservation through breeding and animal releases. Published translocation studies provide examples of how zoo-sourced animals have been involved in wildlife recovery efforts.

FIGURE 04 • ANIMAL GROUP COMPARISON

Which animal groups feature in Australian release studies?

The research dataset contains international studies. This chart includes only records concerning Australia.

KEY FINDING
Mammals lead the Australian release-study sample

Of the 27 Australian study records, 13 concern mammals, seven herpetofauna, six birds and one fish record.

The chart counts study records rather than unique conservation projects or individual animals.

FIGURE 05 • RELEASE OUTCOMES

What happened after release?

Researchers reported successful, unsuccessful, undetermined or unreported outcomes.

KEY FINDING
Outcome uncertainty is a central finding

Among 27 Australian records, 15 are classified Undetermined, nine Success, two Failure, and one has no reported outcome. An undetermined result is not a failure.

An unreported or undetermined outcome does not indicate that a conservation program failed.

03 / INVESTING IN RECOVERY

Conservation requires more than animal care

Australian recovery grants document targeted conservation investment. At a global scale, published zoo-associated release studies provide another view of conservation activity and where it has been researched.

FIGURE 06 • FUNDING COMPARISON

Where was conservation funding allocated?

Compare the funding received by organisations under the 2020 wildlife recovery grants.

KEY FINDING
Funding is concentrated in New South Wales

Across the state-assigned 2020 grants, NSW has A$5.40 million over 10 records, compared with A$1.95 million in Victoria, A$1.00 million in Queensland and A$998,100 in South Australia.

These are grant allocations, not evidence of completed projects or measured conservation success.

04 / ACROSS STATE BOUNDARIES

Threatened wildlife crosses borders

Wildlife does not follow administrative boundaries. The same threatened taxon may be recorded in several Australian states and territories. Examining these overlaps reveals patterns that individual state totals cannot show.

FIGURE 08 • UPSET PLOT

Which state combinations appear most often in threatened-taxa records?

This UpSet plot highlights the 12 most frequent exact state-occurrence combinations. Each column represents one combination: connected dots identify its states, while the bar above shows how many listed taxa occur in exactly that combination.

KEY FINDING
Multiple-state occurrences matter

A taxon can have a Yes flag in more than one jurisdiction. Each UpSet column therefore counts taxa in one exact state combination, rather than simply adding state totals.

HOW TO READ THIS CHART
Bar height = listed taxa State included State not included Connected states form a combination

Source: Australian Government threatened-species records. The chart shows exact state-occurrence combinations among recorded jurisdictions, not migration paths. Taxa with no recorded jurisdiction are excluded.

05 / RESEARCH AND DISTRIBUTION

What the evidence can — and cannot — tell us

Published research documents what happened in studied zoo-associated releases. Threatened-species lists identify states of recorded occurrence. These are different types of evidence and should not be confused.

FIGURE 09 • SANKEY DIAGRAM

How do reported outcomes differ by animal group?

Flows connect animal groups to outcomes reported in Australian zoo-sourced release study records. Wider ribbons represent more study records, not larger numbers of released animals.

KEY FINDING
Published records do not always establish success

Of the 27 Australian study records, 15 have Undetermined outcomes. The Sankey connects animal groups with reported research outcomes, not numbers of released animals.

Source: Blais and colleagues, supplementary translocation data. Counts describe study records. Undetermined and unreported outcomes are not equivalent to failures. Hover over a ribbon for its count.

FIGURE 10 • DOT-DENSITY MAP

How widely are threatened taxa recorded?

Each terracotta dot represents approximately 10 nationally listed taxa recorded in a state or territory. Dots are distributed inside state boundaries for visual comparison.

KEY FINDING
Dots are a comparison device—not sightings

NSW has 287 state-occurrence records and Queensland has 265. Each synthetic dot represents approximately 10 occurrences within its state.

1 dot ≈ 10 threatened-taxa state-occurrence records

One dot ≈ 10 state-occurrence records, rounded to the nearest dot. Dot positions are illustrative, not actual species locations or population distributions. The same taxon can appear in multiple states.

Understanding the scale of extinction risk

Beyond where listed taxa occur, the national records reveal which animal classes contain the largest number of critically endangered taxa and how the overall list is divided by conservation status.

FIGURE 11 • LOLLIPOP CHART

Rank the eight animal classes with the highest counts of nationally listed critically endangered taxa. Hover to compare each class's total listings and its critically endangered share.

KEY FINDING
Reptiles lead the critically endangered count

Reptilia has 27 Critically Endangered listed taxa and Actinopterygii (ray-finned fishes) has 26. The chart compares counts, not extinction probabilities.

Counts are of listed taxa classified as Critically Endangered, not individual animals. The chart ranks absolute counts, not the proportion at risk within each class.

FIGURE 12 • WAFFLE CHART

KEY FINDING
More than two-thirds are Endangered or Vulnerable

Endangered (255) and Vulnerable (221) together account for 476 of 700 listed taxa—68% of the dataset. Each waffle cell represents about 1% of the list.

The 100 squares are rounded using the largest-remainder method. Very small categories may round to zero squares. Source: Australian Government threatened-species records, extracted 28 August 2026.

07 / SOURCES AND METHODOLOGY

Understanding the evidence

This visualisation brings together three published sources covering threatened taxa, zoo-sourced translocation research and government wildlife recovery grants. They measure different phenomena and should not be treated as equivalent observations.

Data sources

Australian threatened animals: Australian Government threatened-species records. Data.gov.au

Zoo-associated conservation translocations: Blais and colleagues, supplementary research data. Research publication

Wildlife rescue and captive-breeding grants: Australian Government bushfire recovery funding. Government funding source

Important limitations

Threatened-animal records concern listed taxa, not individual animals or population abundance. State occurrences overlap because the same taxon may be recorded in multiple jurisdictions.

Published translocation studies do not form a complete census of all zoo programs. Grant funding relates to 2020 and includes organisations other than zoos.

The non-contiguous cartogram encodes published study counts with country area, rather than mapping actual animal movements. The dot-density map uses synthetic within-state positions and does not locate actual animals. Sankey widths encode study records rather than animals released.
